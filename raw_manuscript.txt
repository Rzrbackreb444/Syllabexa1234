## Chapter 3: Location Selection

You can fix equipment. You can renegotiate rent. But you can't fix a bad location.

If you ignore this law, the business gods will punish you every month with low turns and high regret. **Location determines 80% of your success.** Let that sink in. You could have the best equipment, the cleanest store, the friendliest staff - and still fail because you picked the wrong spot.

### What to Look For: The Location Checklist

**Must-Have Criteria:**

| Factor | Min Standard | Ideal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renters (1 mi) | 1,500+ | 3,000+ |
| Visibility | Main road access | Corner lot |
| Parking | 1.5 spaces/wash | 2 spaces/wash |
| Competition | 2 within 1 mi | 0-1 within 1 mi |

### The C.L.E.A.N. Principle

The Kremers doctrine uses **C.L.E.A.N.** for every potential location:

| Letter | Principle | What to Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C | Community | Demographics & density |
| L | Lease Logic | Terms, length, options |
| E | Equipment | Size distribution & mix |
| A | Accessibility | Parking & traffic flow |
| N | Numbers | Rent ratio & EBITDA |

Run these checks before you ever sign a lease.

### Red Flags to Avoid

**Immediate Disqualifiers:**
* Less than 1,000 renters within 1 mile
* Rent exceeds 25% of projected revenue
* No parking or street parking only
* High crime area (check police reports)
* 3+ competitors within 1 mile
* Declining neighborhood (check census trends)

**Warning Signs (Proceed with Caution):**
* Lease term under 5 years
* Landlord resistance to exclusivity clause
* Recent new construction permits nearby
* Changing demographics (gentrification)
* Seasonal population fluctuations
* Major employer in area (single point of failure)

## Chapter 4: The Business Model

Bad leases ruin good businesses. A perfect location with a bad lease is still a ticking time bomb.

I've seen it too many times: an operator finds a great spot, gets excited, signs whatever the landlord puts in front of them, and three years later they're wondering why they can't sell their business.

The answer is always the lease.

### Lease Essentials: What You Must Have

**Non-Negotiable Terms:**

| Term | Min | Why It Matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial Term | 10 Years | Ensures enough time to recover high CAPEX (equipment/build-out). |
| Renewal Options | 2 x 5 Years | Guarantees long-term site control; prevents landlord from holding you hostage. |
| Assignment Clause | Right to Assign | If you can't transfer the lease, you can't sell the business. Essential exit strategy. |
| Exclusivity | Total | Prevents landlord from leasing to a competitor in the same shopping center. |
| Rent Increases | Max 3-5% Cap | Prevents unmanageable overhead spikes that destroy net margins. |
| HVAC Repair | Landlord Responsible | Prevents massive, surprise capital expenses; tenant pays only minor maintenance. |
| Personal Guarantee | 24-Mo Burn-off | Limits your personal asset exposure; guarantee must expire after a track record of reliability. |

### Larry's Advanced Lease Negotiating Tactics

*From Larry Larsen's 50+ years of industry experience*

**Before You Negotiate:**
1. Research the landlord's other properties
2. Know vacancy rates in the area
3. Understand the landlord's motivation
4. Have your financing pre-approved
5. Be willing to walk away

**Key Negotiation Points:**

| Negotiation Point | Landlord Goal | Your Tactical Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base Rent | Maximize profit; lock in escalations | Benchmark market rate; push for annual caps of 3% or CPI-tied only |
| CAM/Expenses | Pass all costs to tenant | Audit history; demand cap on controllable CAM; exclude structural/capital repairs |
| Exclusivity | Keep open for other potential tenants | Non-negotiable; total category exclusivity for laundromat services |
| Maintenance | Tenant handles everything | Landlord maintains roof, structure, and parking lot; cap tenant's HVAC responsibility |
| Exit Strategy | Control over subletting | Right to assign without consent (or reasonable consent); no transfer fees |

> "The best lease is one you are prepared to walk away from. If the landlord won't move on the 'Non-Negotiables,' the deal isn't a bargain—it's a trap."

### The Assignment Clause: Your Exit Strategy

"If you can't assign the lease, you can't sell the business. Period." - Larry Larsen

**What to Negotiate:**
* Right to assign without landlord consent (or with "reasonable" consent)
* No transfer fees or minimal fees
* No requirement to remain personally liable after assignment
* Clear standards for acceptable assignees

### Reading Your Lease: What Most People Miss

**Hidden Costs to Watch For:**
* CAM (Common Area Maintenance) charges
* Property tax pass-throughs
* Insurance requirements beyond standard
* HVAC maintenance and replacement
* Roof repairs
* Parking lot maintenance
* Trash removal

**Calculate True Occupancy Cost:** Base Rent + CAM + Taxes + Insurance + Required Maintenance = True Cost

### Equipment Valuation in a Sale

*Expert valuation standards by Lawrence "Laundromat Larry" Larsen.*

When assessing the value of equipment during a business acquisition or sale, use the following age-based percentage of current replacement cost:

* **0-3 Years (New):** 70-85% of new value. Equipment is typically under warranty with minimal wear.
* **4-7 Years (Mid-Life):** 50-70% of new value. Prime operational years; valuation depends heavily on maintenance logs.
* **8-10 Years (Mature):** 30-50% of new value. Expect increasing repair costs and cosmetic wear.
* **10+ Years (End-of-Life):** 5-25% of new value. Valued primarily as "bridge" equipment until a full retool can be funded.

**Key Valuation Factors:**
* Maintenance records (add 10-15% if documented)
* Cosmetic condition
* Cycle counts if available
* Parts availability
* Brand reputation

### Machine Lifecycle Matrix

Managing your fleet requires different strategies at different ages. Use this lifecycle guide to plan your maintenance budget and capital expenditures:

* **New Phase (0-3 Years):** Focus on warranty validation. Performance tracking is critical to establish a baseline for water and energy efficiency.
* **Mid-Life Phase (4-7 Years):** Implementation of aggressive preventative maintenance. This is the "Profit Peak" where the machine is paid off but still highly efficient.
* **Mature Phase (8-10 Years):** Major component inspection (bearings, seals, motors). Begin a dedicated "Retool Reserve" fund, allocating 10% of gross revenue for future replacement.
* **End-of-Life Phase (10+ Years):** Evaluate the "Repair vs. Replace" threshold. If a single repair exceeds 25% of the machine's remaining value, replace immediately to avoid the "Repair Spiral."

## Chapter 6: Equipment Selection (Softmount vs. Hardmount)

*This chapter draws from Larry Larsen's extensive writings on equipment selection.*

The softmount vs. hardmount debate has divided the industry for decades. Here's the definitive breakdown.

![Figure 6.1: Softmount vs. Hardmount Structural Comparison](/soft vs hard.png)

### What's the Difference?

**Hardmount Washers:**
* Bolted directly to concrete slab
* Require 8-12 inch reinforced concrete pad
* Lower spin speeds (typically 100-200 G-force)
* More water left in clothes after wash

**Softmount Washers:**
* Free-standing on rubber isolators
* No special floor requirements
* Higher spin speeds (300-400+ G-force)
* Clothes come out drier

> "Debt is a tool, not a lifestyle. Use it to build assets that produce cash flow, not liabilities that drain your margin."

## Chapter 7: Utility Infrastructure

Plumbing, electrical, and gas systems are the lifeblood of your operation. Ignore them, and you'll bleed cash through inefficiencies or face catastrophic downtime. 

**Plumbing and Drainage:**
Water is your primary consumable. Always ensure your drains are sized correctly. You need a minimum of 3-inch, but ideally 4-inch drain lines, to handle the rapid discharge of multiple washers spinning out simultaneously. A backed-up drain flooding your store on a Saturday morning is a nightmare you only want to experience once.

**Electrical Capacity:**
Modern equipment requires substantial power. If available, 3-phase power is vastly superior for commercial equipment, offering better efficiency and longevity for your motors. Ensure your panel has at least 20% overhead capacity beyond your maximum calculated load.

**Gas and Make-Up Air:**
Your dryers are useless without adequate gas pressure and, critically, make-up air. For every cubic foot of air your dryers exhaust, a cubic foot must be replaced. Without proper make-up air, dryers will starve, dry times will double, and your utility bills will skyrocket.

## Chapter 8: Design, Layout, and Customer Flow

A laundromat is a production facility masquerading as a retail storefront. Your layout must serve both functions flawlessly.

![Figure 8.1: Equipment Alignment and Design](/Untitled design.png)

**Sightlines and Security:**
Keep the center of the store low. Place your folding tables and smaller washers in the middle aisles, and line the perimeter with your tall, stacked dryers and XL washers. This allows an attendant (or your security cameras) an unobstructed view of the entire facility from any vantage point.

**The Folding Ratio:**
A common amateur mistake is under-allocating folding space. Aim for one folding table for every two to three washers. If customers can't fold comfortably, they will leave wet and frustrated. 

**Aesthetics and Lighting:**
Laundromats of the past were dark, dingy, and intimidating. Modern facilities are bright, welcoming, and safe. Use 4000K to 5000K LED lighting to create a clean, surgical brightness that highlights your stainless steel equipment and spotless floors.

## Chapter 9: Funding the Dream

Debt is a tool, not a lifestyle. Use it to build assets that produce cash flow, not liabilities that drain your margin.

**Financing Options:**
Most operators leverage SBA 7(a) loans or specialized equipment financing (like Dexter Financial or Eastern Funding). SBA loans offer longer amortization periods (up to 10 years for acquisition/build-outs), which keeps monthly payments lower and preserves cash flow.

**Capital Requirements:**
You will generally need 20% to 30% of the total project cost in liquid cash as a down payment. More importantly, never spend your last dollar on the build. You must retain a working capital reserve of $20,000 to $40,000 to cover initial utility deposits, unexpected construction overages, and the slow ramp-up period before the store reaches break-even.

## Appendix D: Water Bill Analysis Tutorial

Water bills are the closest thing to an impartial witness of a laundromat's actual usage.

**The Basic Calculation:**
* **Step 1:** Get 12 months of water bills
* **Step 2:** Calculate average gallons per month
* **Step 3:** Estimate cycles (Average washer uses 15-25 gallons per cycle. Use 20 gallons as a conservative estimate)
* **Step 4:** Calculate revenue: (Monthly gallons ÷ 20) × average vend price = estimated washer revenue
* **Step 5:** Add 30-40% for dryer revenue
* **Step 6:** Compare to seller's claimed revenue

**Example Analysis:**

| Month | Gallons | Est. Cycles | Est. Revenue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Jan | 45,000 | 2,250 | $9,000 |

**If seller claims $15,000/month but water suggests $9,000 - you have a problem.**

**Red Flags:**
* Water usage inconsistent with revenue claims
* Declining water usage trend
* Seasonal patterns that don't match claimed revenue
* Water bills in someone else's name

## Appendix K: First-Time Buyer Q&A

**Q: How much money do I need to buy a laundromat?**
A: Plan for $150K-$400K total for an existing store, including:
* Purchase price (typically $100K-$300K)
* Closing costs ($5K-$15K)
* Initial repairs/improvements ($10K-$50K)
* Working capital reserve ($10K-$30K)
With financing, you may need 20-30% down ($30K-$120K cash).

**Q: Can I do this with no experience?**
A: Yes, but you need to compensate with education. Read this book cover to cover. Visit at least 20 laundromats. Talk to operators. Consider hiring a consultant for your first deal. The investment in knowledge pays dividends.

**Q: How much time does it really take to manage?**
A: First 6-12 months: 15-25 hours/week as you learn and build systems. After systems are in place: 5-15 hours/week. With a manager: 2-5 hours/week oversight. It's not passive income. It's systematized income.

**Q: What's the biggest mistake first-time buyers make?**
A: Paying too much because they fell in love with the idea. The second biggest: not doing proper due diligence. Use our checklist (Appendix C) religiously. Trust the numbers, not the seller's story.

## Appendix L: AI and IoT Implementation Guide

A practical roadmap for integrating smart technology into your laundromat.

**Phase 1: Foundation (Months 1-3)**
* Audit existing equipment for IoT compatibility
* Install cellular or WiFi gateway
* Deploy sensors on highest-revenue machines first
* Set up basic alerting for critical failures

**Phase 2: Customer Experience (Months 4-6)**
* Real-time machine availability
* Push notifications when cycle completes
* Mobile payment integration
* Digital loyalty program

**Phase 3: Intelligence (Months 7-12)**
* Bearing wear: Increasing vibration amplitude over 2-3 weeks
* Belt degradation: Slower spin-up times, inconsistent RPM
* Heating element failure: Temperature variance >15% from baseline
* Water valve issues: Flow rate drops or irregular fill times

* Automated service ticket generation
* Customer service chatbots
* Inventory auto-reordering
* Energy optimization algorithms
